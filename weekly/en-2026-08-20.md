## The Week's Throughline

The week ending August 20 was the week crypto's two eras collided. Bybit suffered the largest exchange theft on record — $1.46 billion — and had to argue its own security controls worked, claiming more than $700 million in additional outflows were blocked. Days earlier, the Coldcard hardware-wallet seed-generation flaw had pushed stolen Bitcoin past $115 million, with Galaxy Research warning losses could exceed $150 million. In the same five days, Washington assembled the regime meant to make such failures rarer: Treasury proposed stablecoin-issuer licensing under the GENIUS Act, the SEC proposed its first comprehensive Regulation Crypto Assets framework plus token-offering exemptions capped at $75 million, and the OCC granted a conditional national trust charter to Trump-linked World Liberty for the USD1 stablecoin. The thefts made the week dramatic; the rulemaking made it structural.

## Continuing Threads

- **Bybit's record hack became a story about containment, not just loss.** Bybit said its controls blocked more than $700 million in potential further outflows after the initial breach, roughly halving the practical damage of the $1.46 billion theft; the mechanism was not disclosed. The incident reset the benchmark for exchange security failures — and for judging an exchange by its emergency response.
- **The Coldcard flaw produced a slow-motion drain, not a one-off heist.** Galaxy tied a July 30 sweep of 1,082.65 BTC from 1,196 addresses to weak randomness in seed generation present since firmware 4.0.1 (March 2021), affecting five models. Losses reached 1,778 BTC (~$112 million), with a possible fourth wave of 638.5 BTC that would push the total above $150 million; roughly 1,531 BTC remains unmoved in attacker-controlled addresses. Attacks slowed after August 6, but as Foundation CEO Zach Herbert put it, reputation is not a security model.
- **US policy moved from enforcement to licensing — with the White House leading the push.** Treasury's GENIUS Act proposal requires stablecoin issuers to hold a federal or state license from January 18, 2027, and restricts platforms from listing unapproved stablecoins after July 18, 2028. The SEC proposed Regulation Crypto Assets and a token-offering package — two exemptions, a conditional safe harbor, a $75 million cap, unanimous commission support — its first comprehensive rulemaking for crypto. The OCC's conditional charter puts USD1 issuance under direct federal banking oversight, Trump personally pressed the Senate to pass the CLARITY Act alongside Coinbase's Brian Armstrong and the Winklevoss twins, and the CFTC is working to bring Hyperliquid into the US. The architecture has shifted; the politics are now the bottleneck.
- **Tether crossed the audit threshold just as the rules tightened.** KPMG issued a clean, unqualified opinion on Tether International's 2025 financials — the first Big Four audit of the ~$180 billion stablecoin issuer — with reserves exceeding liabilities by $6.814 billion, and physically counted the gold backing bar by bar. The timing is the story: the GENIUS Act and FDIC proposals impose stricter reserve and liquidity rules, and the FDIC's eligible-asset menu does not include gold, so the transparency milestone arrived exactly as the compliance bar moved higher.

## Worth Remembering

- **Maya Protocol's $1.7 million exploit was the smallest loss and the most instructive.** Post-mortems found six bugs let the attacker inflate a pool by 49.45 million CACAO and take 99.93% control before a global halt; total pool value fell $11 million. Combined with Harmony's decision to roll back its chain over 3 trillion forged ONE tokens and Bedrock's $2 million heist, the week showed cross-chain liquidity protocols remain crypto's softest target — and that immutability is now negotiable when a chain is hit.
- **The centralized-exchange shakeout is accelerating.** ABFinance became the fourth exchange to shut down in six weeks, after BitMart, BitMEX, and AscendEX, and never launched; BitMart's founder dismissed audit demands as users reported blocked withdrawals. Binance moved to block transactions with HTX and ten other unnamed exchanges under EU Russia sanctions, and Protos tracked hundreds of millions in HTX assets — including WBTC and sUSDS — flowing to Justin Sun-owned Poloniex despite HTX being under EU and UK sanctions. Solvency and sanctions compliance are now the two filters deciding which custodians survive.
- **Binance handed Russia the data that put a Ukraine donor on trial.** An exclusive report documented that Binance provided Russian security services with client data later used to charge a Russian user for donating to Ukraine's military; the user is now on trial. Binance says it cooperates with lawful requests even after exiting the Russian market in 2023. This is the case that will define how regulators weigh exchange compliance against user privacy.
- **Aave's book is one crowded trade away from a cascade.** Galaxy's August 7 snapshot found fewer than 9% of Aave V3 Core loans (E-mode borrowers) carry roughly half of all outstanding debt, concentrated in leveraged Ethereum staking-basis positions with an average health factor near 1.06. An 8–9% discount on liquid staking tokens relative to ETH could trigger cascading liquidations; Galaxy's earlier stress test estimated a 10% weETH depeg would push 205 accounts below health factor 1. The risk is known, priced, and continually rolled — until it isn't.
